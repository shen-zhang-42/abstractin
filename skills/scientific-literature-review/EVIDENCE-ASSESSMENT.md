# Evidence synthesis and assessment

Read this file whenever the skill classifies papers, constructs synthesis units, rates empirical evidence, evaluates replication, or synthesizes conflict.

## Core distinction

The review's primary unit is the **review-relevant synthesis unit**.

A synthesis unit is conditioned on the current review question. It does not have to be the source paper's own main conclusion.

For every paper ask:

> What information from this paper materially contributes to the current review question?

---

# Concept structure vs synthesis units

The conceptual structure and the synthesis-unit set are related but distinct.

The conceptual structure tracks five classes of review-relevant concepts:

- question;
- entity / phenomenon;
- theory / explanation;
- method;
- relation / boundary.

A synthesis unit is an assessable statement used to answer the review question.

One concept may generate multiple synthesis units, and one synthesis unit may connect multiple concepts.

Do not treat search aliases, terminology variants, author clusters, or citation clusters as conceptual nodes unless they correspond to a scientifically meaningful distinction.

---

# 1. Paper importance

Paper importance is relative to the current review question.

Do not equate prestige or citation count with importance.

Score papers on five dimensions when useful:

## Conceptual importance
Does the paper introduce or substantially change a concept, theory, mechanism, model, or paradigm?

## Topical centrality
How directly does the paper address the current review question?

## Citation-network centrality
Does the paper occupy an important network position or connect major branches?

## Citation impact
How influential is the paper by citation count, accounting for age and field when possible?

## Evidential importance
Does the paper provide decisive evidence, replication, contradiction, comparison, or a critical methodological contribution?

Topical, conceptual, and evidential importance should normally outweigh raw citation count.

Classify papers as:

### Core
Important for the structure of the review or for evaluating a major synthesis unit.

### Supporting
Directly supports, defines, compares, replicates, extends, limits, or contradicts a major synthesis unit.

### Peripheral
Relevant but not important for the major synthesis units.

Core papers are the main starting points for citation expansion.

---

# 2. Synthesis-unit construction

Use review-relevant synthesis units rather than paper summaries.

Common unit types:

### Empirical claim
A claim about an observed relationship, effect, mechanism, prediction, or outcome.

### Theoretical or formal claim
A proposition, model property, formal relationship, or theoretical distinction.

### Method / model characterization
A description of what a method/model contains, assumes, estimates, measures, or is used for.

### Comparative result
A comparison between models, methods, theories, or procedures.

### Boundary / limitation
A condition under which a claim, model, or method does or does not hold.

### Other review-relevant information
Any other source-grounded unit necessary to answer the review question.

A synthesis unit should be concise enough to assess but broad enough to avoid trivial fragmentation.

Keep units separate when they differ substantially in:

- population;
- mechanism;
- manipulation;
- outcome;
- paradigm;
- causal status;
- model or method;
- boundary condition;
- inferential basis.

For broad topics, group synthesis units under themes.

---

# 3. Source depth is not evidence strength

Every important extracted contribution should retain the source depth used to verify it:

- `metadata`
- `abstract`
- `partial-text`
- `full-text`

Source depth answers:

> How deeply was this information checked in the source paper?

Evidence strength answers:

> How strongly does the literature support this empirical claim?

Do not confuse the two.

A full-text-verified result can still be weak evidence if it is a single unreplicated study.

An abstract-level description can be sufficient for a simple bibliographic or explicitly stated fact, but not for details the abstract does not report.

---

# 4. When to use E0–E4

Use E0–E4 only when the synthesis unit is an **empirical claim for which replication-based evidence strength is meaningful**.

Do not force descriptive method facts, model definitions, or formal identities into E0–E4.

For non-empirical units, use:

- source verification;
- agreement / disagreement across sources when relevant;
- formal assumptions or derivation basis;
- known scope or boundary conditions;
- unresolved alternatives.

If a method or model itself is being empirically evaluated, the resulting performance claim may use E0–E4.

Example:

- "CPT includes a value function and probability-weighting function" → descriptive/model characterization; E-level not applicable.
- "CPT predicts choices better than EU in population X" → empirical comparative claim; E-level applicable.

---

# 5. Empirical evidence levels

## E0 — Unsupported
No convincing direct empirical support, or available evidence is strongly undermined by contradictory evidence.

## E1 — Single-line evidence
Evidence comes mainly from one research group or closely related research lineage.

Repeated findings from the same laboratory remain E1.

## E2 — Independently replicated
At least one credible independent research group has successfully replicated the core finding.

## E3 — Robustly replicated
Multiple independent groups reproduce the finding and the evidence is broadly consistent across studies.

## E4 — Established
Evidence is stable across independent groups, methods, samples, and relevant conditions, and major alternative explanations have been substantially constrained.

---

# 6. Replication hierarchy

For empirical claims, evaluate evidence in this order:

1. independent replication;
2. within-group replication;
3. individual-study quality.

Independent replication normally outweighs the statistical strength of any single study.

Multiple papers from the same research lineage must not be counted as equivalent to multiple independent replications.

Record when relevant:

### Original-team replication
- none
- partial
- repeated

### Independent replication
- none
- successful
- mixed
- failed
- opposite result

---

# 7. Conflict

For empirical claims, evidence level and conflict are separate dimensions.

Use:

- None
- Mild
- Major

Independent contradictory evidence receives greater diagnostic weight than additional positive reports from the originating group.

When judging failed replication or opposite evidence, check comparability:

- population;
- protocol;
- measurement;
- statistical power;
- implementation quality;
- model specification when relevant.

A reliable independent opposite result normally implies major conflict.

Do not infer misconduct without direct evidence.

For non-empirical synthesis units, do not mechanically apply the empirical conflict scale. Instead describe competing definitions, assumptions, formulations, or interpretations directly.

---

# 8. Individual-study quality

After replication structure has been evaluated for empirical claims, consider:

- sample size;
- statistical power;
- effect size;
- uncertainty;
- robustness;
- experimental design;
- preregistration;
- multiple-comparison control;
- measurement validity;
- model comparison quality;
- alternative explanations.

Do not allow a statistically impressive single study to compensate for lack of independent replication.

---

# 9. Publication bias

For empirical claims, actively search for:

- failed replications;
- null findings;
- contradictory findings;
- registered reports;
- preregistered studies;
- replication projects.

Credible independent null or contradictory evidence may carry substantial diagnostic weight because positive findings are generally easier to publish.

---

# 10. Required synthesis-unit fields

For every major synthesis unit report:

## ID and statement
A concise review-relevant statement.

## Type
One of:

- empirical claim;
- theoretical/formal claim;
- method/model characterization;
- comparative result;
- boundary/limitation;
- other review-relevant information.

## Review relevance
Why this unit matters for the current review question.

## Source basis
Which papers provide, support, define, qualify, or challenge the unit.

## Source depth
The verification depth of the important paper-level information used.

## Evidence assessment
If empirical:

- E0–E4;
- conflict;
- original-team replication;
- independent replication;
- why this level.

If non-empirical:

- write `Evidence level: N/A` when needed by the report schema;
- state verification basis, formal basis, competing formulations, or uncertainty instead.

## Boundary conditions
Specify populations, paradigms, methods, assumptions, or conditions where the unit applies.

## Unresolved questions
State remaining scientific or interpretive uncertainty specific to this unit.

---

# 11. Cross-cutting issues

Only create a separate section when an unresolved issue affects multiple synthesis units.

Examples:

- common measurement problem;
- publication bias;
- shared sampling limitation;
- methodological dependency;
- lack of independent laboratories;
- model-identification problem;
- ambiguity in terminology or definitions.

---

# 12. Information-extraction interface

When a synthesis unit requires information not available from metadata or abstract, use `scientific-information-extraction`.

The extraction result should preserve:

- `answer`;
- `evidence[]`;
- `source_locations[]`;
- `source_depth`;
- `inference` when depth 1 is used;
- `limitations`.

Use these fields as paper-level inputs to review synthesis.

Do not promote a one-step paper-level inference into a literature-wide conclusion without cross-paper synthesis.
